How accessible are Android apps built for blind users?

DiscoverABILITY Lab, University of North Texas, May 2025 to January 2026. With Dr. Stephanie Ludi (principal investigator), Dr. Rana Ghoneim and Dr. Wajdi Aljedaani.

Manuscript under review

Question
Apps made for blind and visually impaired people should be the most accessible apps there are. Are they?
Method
We looked at 26 such apps in two ways: an automated scan of their screens, and what people wrote about them in 8,286 Google Play reviews.
My part
I ran Google Accessibility Scanner on the onboarding, interactive and feedback screens and sorted 1,810 violations into 12 kinds, such as touch target size, contrast and focus order. I built the Python pipelines that classify the reviews, and helped relate what the scanner flags to what reviewers describe.
Limits
The manuscript is under review at Universal Access in the Information Society, so the findings are not published yet. Reviews are public Google Play reviews; we cannot confirm who wrote them.

Do reproduction steps change how an AI triages an accessibility bug report?

TalkBack Triage Pilot, November 2026. An independent follow-up to the lab study, which I proposed to Dr. Wajdi Aljedaani.

Exploratory: one AI run, no human labels yet

Question
When a bug report includes the steps to reproduce a problem with TalkBack, Android’s screen reader, does a large language model judge the report differently?
Method
I built a web app around 75 real accessibility issues from two open-source Android apps. For each of the 32 reports that have TalkBack steps, it makes a copy with only those steps removed, and asks the model the same two questions about both versions.
Result
Removing the steps changed the model’s answer to “needs more evidence” on 12 reports, and the other way on 1 (exact McNemar test, p = 0.003).
Limits
This shows the model’s answer depends on those lines. It does not show the answer is right. That needs people to label the same reports, which the app is built for and which has not happened yet. The app’s code was written with AI assistance, as the README states.

Can a drone’s flight log prove that nobody changed it?

flightseal, November 2026. C++17 with OpenSSL (SHA-256 and Ed25519).

Open source, built with AI assistance

Question
After a crash, the flight log is evidence. Can the log itself show whether someone edited it, even if that person holds the file and can rewrite all of it?
Method
Each record carries a checksum and joins a SHA-256 hash chain. Every few thousand records, a signed receipt goes to someone else, such as an investigator or an insurer.
Result
On a public PX4 flight log of 64,542 records, the tool catches a single edited altitude reading, a full rewrite of the chain, and a log with its last 2,000 records deleted.
Limits
A demonstration on one public log, not a system that flies. The code was written with AI assistance, as the README states.

An open dataset of accessibility bug reports

a11y-issue-warehouse, November 2026.

Public on GitHub, labels not hand-verified

2,643 issues from 15 open-source Android apps: 1,187 accessibility reports and a date-matched comparison sample, in a tested SQLite warehouse with 19 data-quality checks. One check caught 180 search results that were not about accessibility at all.

A text classifier, scored on each app after training only on the other 14, reaches F1 0.756 with the defining keywords hidden (95% bootstrap interval 0.736 to 0.776). The README lists what the data can and cannot show.

  • SQL (SQLite)
  • scikit-learn
